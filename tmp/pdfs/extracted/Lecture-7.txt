1 Why We Need Relative Measures of Dispersion
Absolute measures of dispersion (Range, Mean Deviation, Standard Deviation) are useful
for comparing datasets with the same units and similar means. However, they have
limitations when comparing datasets with different units or different magnitudes.
1.1 The Problem with Absolute Measures
Why Absolute Measures Fail
Consider two datasets representing response times from two different systems:
System A:100, 102, 104, 106, 108 (Mean = 104 ms, SD = 3.16 ms)
System B:1000, 1020, 1040, 1060, 1080 (Mean = 1040 ms, SD = 31.6 ms)
System Mean (ms) Standard Deviation (ms) Interpretation
A 104 3.16 Low variability?
B 1040 31.6 High variability?
Question:Which system is more consistent?
Common Mistake:Looking only at SD, one might say System A is more con-
sistent (3.16 < 31.6).
But wait!
•System A: SD = 3.16 out of 104 = about 3% variation
•System B: SD = 31.6 out of 1040 = about 3% variation
•Both have the SAME relative variation!
Conclusion:We need RELATIVE measures to compare datasets with different
scales or units.
1.2 When to Use Relative Measures
Real-World Scenarios
•Comparing Different Units:Compare stock price volatility (in Rs) with
company earnings (in Rs Crores)
•Comparing Different Scales:Compare response times (ms) with throughput
(requests/sec)
•Comparing Different Magnitudes:Compare variability in small datasets
vs large datasets
•Quality Control:Compare consistency across different production lines
•Performance Monitoring:Compare reliability across different systems

2 Relative Measures of Dispersion
Relative measures are expressed as ratios or percentages, making them unit-free and
comparable across different datasets.
2.1 1. Coefficient of Range
Coefficient of Range= Maximum−Minimum
Maximum+Minimum
2.1.1 Example: Computing Coefficient of Range
Dataset A:100, 101, 102, 103, 104
Coefficient of Range= 104−100
104 + 100 = 4
204 = 0.0196
Dataset B:1000, 1010, 1020, 1030, 1040
Coefficient of Range= 1040−1000
1040 + 1000 = 40
2040 = 0.0196
Interpretation:Both datasets have the SAME relative range (1.96%).

2.3 3. Coefficient of Variation (CV)
Coefficient of Variation (CV)=Standard Deviation
Mean ×100%
The Coefficient of Variation (CV) is the MOST commonly used relative measure of
dispersion.
2.3.1 Properties of CV
Key Properties of CV:
•Unit-free:Expressed as a percentage
•Comparable:Can compare across different datasets
•Relative Variability:Measures variability relative to the mean
•Scale Independent:Multiplying all values by a constant doesn’t change CV
•Useful for:Comparing precision, consistency, and risk
2.3.2 Example: Computing Coefficient of Variation
System A:100, 102, 104, 106, 108
•Mean = 104
•SD = 3.16
•CV = 3.16
104 ×100% = 3.04%
System B:1000, 1020, 1040, 1060, 1080
•Mean = 1040
•SD = 31.6
•CV = 31.6
1040 ×100% = 3.04%
Interpretation:Both systems have the SAME coefficient of variation (3.04%), indi-
cating equal relative variability.
Why CV is Important - Real World Example
Suppose you are comparing two investments:
Investment Mean Return Standard Deviation CV
Stock A 10% 2% 20%
Stock B 20% 4% 20%
Question:Which stock has more risk?
Absolute Analysis:Stock B has higher SD (4% vs 2%)→More risk?
Relative Analysis (CV):Both have CV = 20%→Same relative risk!
Conclusion:Stock B has higher returns with proportionally higher risk. The

4.1 Types of Skewness
Type Relationship Shape
Symmetric Mean = Median = Mode Bell-shaped
Positively Skewed (Right) Mean > Median > Mode Long right tail
Negatively Skewed (Left) Mean < Median < Mode Long left tail
4.2 Karl Pearson’s Coefficient of Skewness
Karl Pearson’s Coefficient of Skewness is the most widely used measure of skewness.
Sk= Mean−Mode
Standard Deviation
Alternative Formula (Using Median):
If mode is not well defined:
Sk= 3(Mean−Median)
Standard Deviation
4.2.1 Properties of Karl Pearson’s Coefficient
Key Properties:
•Range:Usually between -3 and +3
•Sk = 0:Perfectly symmetric distribution
•Sk > 0:Positively skewed (right-skewed)
•Sk < 0:Negatively skewed (left-skewed)
•Magnitude:Larger absolute value indicates more skewness
•Unit-free:Can compare skewness across different datasets

6 Key Takeaways for CSE Students
•Why Relative Measures:
–Allow comparison across different units and scales
–Provide unit-free measures of variability
–Essential for comparing different systems
•Coefficient of Variation (CV):
–Most commonly used relative measure
–CV = (SD/Mean)×100%
–Higher CV = More relative variability
–Critical for comparing systems with different scales
•Karl Pearson’s Skewness:
–Measures asymmetry of distribution
–Sk = 0: Symmetric
–Sk > 0: Positively skewed (right)
–Sk < 0: Negatively skewed (left)
–Range: Usually between -3 and +3
•Algorithm Considerations:
–CV: O(N) time, O(1) memory
–Karl Pearson Skewness: O(N) time, O(1) memory
–Bowley’s Skewness: O(N log N) time (sorting required)
